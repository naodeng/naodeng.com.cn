Distributed QA does not have one standard shape. The right operating model depends on where people work, how much their hours overlap, which organizations share the work, and what rules apply to the product. What travels well across those boundaries is a clear agreement about ownership, evidence, handoffs, and decisions.

This article expands the notes from Chris Faraglia’s July 2024 Ministry of Testing session, *Managing Distributed QA Teams: Strategies for Success*. Faraglia is a TestRail Solutions Architect with more than 15 years of enterprise software development, integration, and testing experience across nuclear power and healthcare IT. The supplied notes describe teams he led across the United States, Central Europe, and Southwest Asia. The session draws on the third edition of TestRail’s Software Testing & Quality Report. Speaker recommendations, report findings, and additional implementation ideas are kept distinct below.

## Background and the numbers behind the discussion

The report contains 4,214 responses overall, but each question has its own response count. Among 2,195 respondents answering about external testing partners, 60% said they worked with at least one outside organization. In the release-cadence question, 69% reported shipping at least monthly; 22% reported bi-weekly releases, while weekly and monthly releases each accounted for 18%.

The report also found that 79% of 2,213 respondents conduct root-cause analysis on escaped defects. For test definition, QA/Testers averaged 4.6 on a five-point frequency scale. Non-QA roles averaged 2.4 out of 5—roughly “about half the time”—among 2,261 responses. The group includes developers, product managers, implementation managers, DevOps, UX/UI, business analysts, compliance teams, and external partners. That figure measures how often these roles help define tests; it is not a measure of formal test-review participation.

These numbers describe survey responses, not every QA organization. Their useful message is more modest: external partners and frequent releases are common in this sample, and test work already crosses role boundaries. Teams need explicit coordination because the work is shared—not because a particular org chart is universally correct.

## Four models, not one definition of “distributed”

The session separates work arrangement from geographic spread. “Hybrid” describes a mix of office and remote work; “remote” describes where people work. “Distributed” and “centralized” describe whether people span regions or share a time zone.

| Model | Work arrangement | Geographic pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hybrid Distributed | A mix of office-based and remote work | People span multiple regions or time zones |
| Remote Distributed | Everyone works remotely | People span multiple regions or time zones |
| Remote Centralized | Everyone works remotely | People share one time zone, even if they live in different places |
| Hybrid Centralized | A mix of office, home, or flexible work | People share one region or time zone |

The distinction changes how a team coordinates. A centralized hybrid team may solve a problem in a short live meeting. A distributed team may need an asynchronous decision record, a named handoff owner, and a clear deadline before the next region starts.

## Where distributed QA teams lose time

Time zones do not automatically cause quality problems. Unrecorded decisions and unclear ownership do. A few familiar examples:

- **Urgent work cannot swarm quickly.** A hotfix may need people from several regions, but their working hours barely overlap. Without a rota, escalation path, and release coverage plan, the incident waits for the next handoff.
- **Teams duplicate lifecycle work.** Two regions can discover the same issue, open separate defects, or record different build and test details. Then the team spends time reconciling its own records.
- **Responsibility becomes a blame boundary.** “My overnight pipeline passed; someone else broke it during the day” treats quality as individual defense instead of shared product work. The team still needs named decision owners, though—shared responsibility cannot mean nobody decides.
- **Velocity becomes difficult to plan.** Multiple teams may use different assumptions about what is complete, wait for answers across time zones, or compete for the same environment. The resulting rework makes release and sprint forecasts less reliable.
- **Compliance duties cross borders.** Regulated teams may answer to different standards, data-protection rules, and residency requirements. Healthcare information may bring HIPAA obligations in the United States; other regions and sectors may have their own requirements. Disaster-recovery replication and test/production configuration across cloud availability zones—for example, in AWS or GCP—need security and compliance review, not informal agreement in a chat thread.

The practical target is not zero delay. It is a visible next owner, a known source of truth, enough evidence to continue, and a way to escalate when a decision cannot wait.

## Team Working Agreements make expectations portable

Chris recommends that a team create its working agreements when it forms, then revisit them in retrospectives. The people who depend on the agreement—including external partners—should help write it. Otherwise the document describes what one group wants, not how the work actually moves.

Cover decision rights and role boundaries; how requirements, code, and tests are reviewed; which approvals are needed before production; how leave and backup coverage work around a release; which channels carry decisions; and how test data and environments are reserved. In a shift-left team, everyone can help test. That does not make everyone accountable for every decision: name who owns each acceptance, risk, and release call.

Treat the agreement as a living document. If a release leaves one person covering the whole team, add a coverage rule. If a handoff repeatedly loses context, specify what the sender must record. Review these rules in a retrospective, assign an owner, and set a date to check whether the change helped.

## Definition of Done and Shift Left: make completion observable

A Definition of Done works in Agile and non-Agile teams. It gives people a shared, observable answer to “What evidence means this work is complete?” Build the checklist together and tailor its thresholds to the product. For example, a team might require the pipeline to pass, smoke tests to run against the named build, unit coverage to exceed its agreed threshold (for example, `> X%`), and the product team to complete exploratory testing for the relevant risks.

Avoid criteria such as “the product is user-friendly” unless the team defines what that means, for whom, and how it will be checked. A Definition of Done is not a substitute for release approval, and a coverage percentage by itself does not prove that the feature is safe to release.

Shift Left supports this agreement in three ways. First, clarify requirements early enough for people in every region to review them—async comments and a recorded decision can supplement a meeting. Second, make designs testable: stable identifiers and clear states help automation; dynamically generated or unstable IDs turn routine UI changes into broken tests. Third, shorten feedback loops so that a missed requirement or failing check reaches the people who can act on it while the work is still easy to change.

## Product and QA should refine requirements together

The session’s leading collaboration strategy is to review functional and non-functional requirements with the whole team during grooming, planning, or refinement. Product owners, business stakeholders, QA, development, and operations can surface different gaps: an unclear user outcome, a missing error path, an accessibility expectation, a performance limit, or a compliance constraint.

For a distributed team, the review needs an artifact that survives the meeting. Link the requirement to its acceptance criteria, open questions, risks, and test evidence. If a decision changes later, update the same record and make the change visible to the regions that have not started work yet.

## Put QA technical debt in a real backlog

QA technical debt includes broken tests, missing automation coverage, checks that repeatedly fail for non-product reasons, and defects that escaped to production. Keep the items visible in the product or engineering backlog, give them owners, and discuss priority with the people who own the product risk. If work is not recorded, it is difficult to explain the cost of leaving it undone.

Three practices from the session are useful, with different trade-offs:

1. **Maintain an honest debt list.** Record broken or unreliable tests, gaps in important coverage, and production issues that indicate a missed risk. Include evidence and impact, not just a label saying “technical debt.”
2. **Mark automation candidates.** Identify manual checks that are repeated, have stable rules, and protect meaningful risk. Track them in the backlog—through a TestRail field, a code tag, or a linked Jira item—then revisit whether automating them is worthwhile.
3. **Consider defect tickets for important flaky tests.** Treat a critical test like a product capability when its failure can undermine confidence in release integrity. A bug or task makes ownership and triage visible. This is debated: ticketing every intermittent test can swamp the defect queue and blur product bugs with test-infrastructure work. Use the approach where impact, recurrence, and an owner justify the extra workflow.

## Defect triage needs requirement-to-test traceability

Run defect triage with product or business partners weekly or every other week, depending on release pace and risk. For each report, make it possible to answer: Which feature and requirement does this concern? Which scenario ran? Which test execution failed? What build, data, and environment were involved? That context helps the group distinguish a product defect from a test, data, or environment problem.

Chris describes adding a custom `Triaged` field in Jira. A `Yes` means the item has been discussed with the business and has a decision; a blank value signals that someone still needs to ask what should happen. The exact field is optional. The underlying rule is not: preserve the decision, owner, next action, and links to the requirement and failed execution.

## Test reviews matter more when more roles contribute

The survey’s test-definition data shows QA/Testers at 4.6 out of 5 and non-QA roles at 2.4 out of 5 for how often they define tests. That is a positive shift-left signal, but the report does not measure whether those tests receive a formal review—or whether their quality is consistent. More contributors make clear review criteria more important.

Put test-case review in the working agreement and treat it as a gate before a test is finalized or the related code is merged. Review whether the scenario maps to a requirement, covers the intended risk, uses clear expected results, avoids duplication, and can be understood by someone in another region. TestRail can record status, assignee, and review comments; GitHub or GitLab can capture test changes in a PR/MR; a Slack channel can notify reviewers with a direct link. The tool should make the review visible, not turn it into a rubber stamp.

## Environment Claims prevent teams from testing the wrong build

Chris uses **Environment Claims** to mean an explicit record of who has an environment, what they are using it for, and which build is deployed. It is his term in the session, not a universal industry standard.

Consider the failure mode: an overnight team deploys a hotfix to a shared test environment. The day team assumes that environment still contains the month-end release candidate. They run the wrong tests against the wrong code, report misleading failures, and spend time untangling the mismatch. The fix is operational: assign an environment owner, record its purpose and build, and agree whether a team must update the claim, notify others, or ask before replacing a deployment.

Confluence or a simple shared sheet can hold the claim. The tool matters less than whether everyone can see the current owner, deployed version, intended use, and change window. Add operating-system and browser configuration when those affect the result.

**Milestone Promotion** extends that traceability along the deployment path—development, test, milestone, staging, and production. The names and gates vary by team. At each stage, record whether every required test ran and whether it passed, failed, or was not run; connect those results to the build, environment, operating system, and browser configuration. TestRail custom fields can track location and configuration; a spreadsheet can work for a small pilot, though it is harder to maintain. Shared visibility is the requirement.

## Continuous improvement: root causes and One Plus One Meetings

The report found that 79% of 2,213 respondents said they perform root-cause analysis on escaped defects. That tells us analysis is reported; it does not prove that teams act on what they learn. A review should end with a change, an owner, and a follow-up—not only a category assigned to the incident.

Chris’s **One Plus One Meetings** are one-to-one conversations that work like a personal retrospective. They give people who miss a cross-time-zone team retrospective—or who find it hard to raise an issue in a group—a quieter place to be specific. The conversation asks about one thing the team could improve, one thing it already does well, and one action to take next. The action can belong to the team or to the individual, such as setting up focused Cypress learning.

Ground the improvement point in objective evidence such as reports or metrics, name who will act, and check progress later. The point is to make it easier to raise hard feedback and to help the individual and team improve. If the conversation becomes a blame exercise, reset its purpose before continuing.

## Upskilling needs time and a measurable goal

Chris suggests considering 5–10% of sprint capacity for learning. Treat that as a proposal to discuss, not a universal benchmark; staffing, release pressure, and team needs differ.

“Watch some YouTube” is activity, not a learning outcome. Agree on something people can demonstrate: finish a course module, earn a relevant certification, complete a Cypress exercise, pass a skills assessment, or solve a real issue with a senior teammate. TestRail Academy and Ministry of Testing are possible resources; the session described TestRail Academy as free, but access and terms can change. Pairing and mentoring are especially useful when a teammate is disengaged because of a specific skills gap.

## Q&A: why reasonable processes still fail

**How do you persuade people who do not value working agreements or a Definition of Done?** Connect the agreement to an actual result from a retrospective or root-cause review—a missed approval, duplicate defect, unknown build, or absent backup. Show which specific rule could have reduced the impact, then use internal examples and training to build support. That helps when the quality advocate is the only person asking for the change.

**What if someone focuses only on assigned tasks and avoids wider test discussions?** Use a One Plus One conversation to understand the cause. It may be frustration, a skill gap, low confidence, or a meeting format that excludes the person. Targeted training or pairing with a senior colleague may help more than simply demanding participation; Chris said mentoring had worked particularly well.

**What if another distributed team ignores an agreement?** They may not have helped make it, so they do not feel ownership. Bring internal and partner teams into the decision on equal terms, using a Scrum of Scrums, an all-hands forum, or a joint retrospective. Keep communicating the agreement as people join and leave. Otherwise a legacy decision can outlive its rationale—and new teammates will not know it exists.

**How do you keep continuous improvement from turning into blame?** Set expectations before the conversation: the goal is individual growth and a stronger team. Discuss evidence and actions, not assumed motives. Blame helps neither the person nor the team; clear framing makes it easier to talk about a difficult result without turning it into a trial.

**How should a team with immature processes and uncertain environments communicate?** Less is more. Do not duplicate the same update across email, Teams, Slack, a Scrum board, and several tickets. Use a test-management system as the central record if the team already has one, with only a small number of supporting channels. If there is no central tool, keep the process simple instead of adding more tools to a process that is already hard to follow.

## AI-assisted distributed QA: practical extensions, not webinar claims

The following ideas extend the session’s operating principles; they are not workflows Faraglia presented as implemented features. AI can help distributed work move between regions, but it should preserve evidence and ownership rather than invent certainty.

- **Asynchronous handoffs:** draft a summary of the build, completed checks, open risks, decisions, owner, and next deadline. Link every statement to its source and list unanswered questions separately.
- **Duplicate-defect discovery:** suggest possible duplicates by comparing symptoms, requirements, build, and test execution. A person in triage confirms whether they are the same issue.
- **Test-review assistance:** flag missing expected results, unclear steps, unlinked requirements, or likely duplicate cases. The designated reviewer still approves the test.
- **Release-risk summaries:** combine test results, environment claims, open defects, and milestone status. Separate confirmed evidence from assumptions, stale data, and unknowns.
- **Team knowledge search:** make current agreements, environment changes, and past decisions easier to find. Keep access controls and data-handling rules in force, especially when regulated or customer data is involved.

Do not let an AI summary silently become the source of truth. Keep the underlying record, identify who owns the decision, and make corrections visible to every affected region.

## A four-week pilot and two reusable templates

A small pilot can test whether these practices reduce coordination failures without trying to standardize the whole organization at once.

| Week | Focus | Deliverable and signal to inspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Agree on roles, handoffs, and completion evidence | A team-authored working agreement and Definition of Done; baseline handoff delay and duplicate-defect reports |
| 2 | Make environment use visible | Claims for the shared test environments; inspect wrong-build incidents and blocked environment requests |
| 3 | Add triage and test-review routines | Linked requirements, test runs, and defect decisions; inspect review rework and unresolved ownership |
| 4 | Review the evidence and choose the next change | A retrospective or One Plus One follow-up with owners and dates; decide what to keep, change, or stop |

These are starter templates created to make the session’s ideas usable. Adapt them with the people who will rely on them.

## Summary and references

The session’s closing points are straightforward: maintain shared working agreements; use a Definition of Done the team can verify; keep QA debt in a visible backlog; make environment claims and deployed builds clear; review tests before they become trusted evidence; and give people a practical way to reflect across time zones.

None of these practices depends on having the same team structure everywhere. They depend on making decisions, ownership, and evidence visible to the people who need them. Add automation or AI only where it helps that information move without taking control away from the team.
